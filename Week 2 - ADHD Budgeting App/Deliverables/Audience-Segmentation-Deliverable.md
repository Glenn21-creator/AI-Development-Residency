# ADHD Budgeting App: Audience Segmentation Deliverable

AI Development Residency, Week 2. This deliverable documents the target audience for the ADHD budgeting app in three parts: the audience segmentation, the persona analysis, and the scenario-based use cases.

---

## 1. Audience Segmentation

Possible contributing segments about the target audiences that may play a role in the product. These are working assumptions to refine through continued research, and they feed directly into the unique value proposition.

### Geographic segments

- **Country / region by ADHD awareness and diagnosis rates.** The US, UK, Canada, and Australia have higher adult ADHD diagnosis rates and much more public awareness.
- **Urban vs. rural / high cost-of-living areas.** Dense urban areas have more impulse-spend triggers (delivery apps, rideshare, constant subscriptions), which can make the pain sharper and the product more compelling.

### Behavioral segments

- **Impulse and "doom" spenders** who buy for the dopamine hit and regret it later.
- **Subscription forgetters** who sign up and never cancel, bleeding money on things they do not use.
- **Financial avoiders** who do not open their banking app for weeks because checking the balance causes anxiety.
- **Late payers** driven by time blindness and forgetfulness rather than lack of funds, racking up late fees (the classic "ADHD tax").
- **Serial app abandoners** who have tried Mint, YNAB, or a spreadsheet and quit because the upkeep was too high. This is a huge one. They have already self-identified the problem and are actively unsatisfied.

### Demographic segments

- **Young adults, roughly 18 to 34.** This group drove the recent surge in adult ADHD diagnoses and is the most comfortable adopting a new app.
- **Adults diagnosed later in life (35 to 50+).** A rapidly growing group, especially those who struggled for years before getting answers.
- **Students and early-career workers** managing money seriously for the first time.
- **Parents with ADHD** juggling household finances, and sometimes managing family budgets while also raising neurodivergent kids.
- **Broader neurodivergent users** (autistic folks, AuDHD overlap) who face related executive-function challenges.

### Socioeconomic segments

- **Lower-to-middle income users** who feel budgeting pain most acutely, though you have to price in a way they can still afford.
- **Irregular-income earners:** freelancers, gig workers, and creatives. ADHD folks disproportionately land in these flexible careers, and variable income makes traditional monthly budgeting especially hard.
- **Students** with tight, limited budgets.

## 2. Persona Analysis

Three target personas built from the segmentation, each defined by motivation, situational goal, and life goal.

### Persona 1: Students (18s to 24s) managing their own money for the first time

- **Motivation:** Make limited money last so they never hit the point of going hungry, and do it without having to ask anyone for help. Independence and self-respect are the real drivers, not just the numbers.
- **Situational goal:** Keep a clear, real-time picture of where money is going and take care of the essentials (food, rent) before anything else.
- **Life goal:** Come out of this stage genuinely able to run their own money, carrying real habits and a sense of control into independent adult life.

### Persona 2: Parents (30s to 40s) holding the household

- **Motivation:** Provide stability and make sure their slips (a forgotten bill, an impulse buy) do not hurt the people depending on them. A big part is not feeling like the unreliable partner, and reducing money arguments at home.
- **Situational goal:** Keep every bill paid on time and avoid late fees and overdrafts, run the household budget without it eating all their limited focus, and ideally share visibility with a partner so they are not carrying it alone.
- **Life goal:** Lasting financial security for the family, saving toward their kids' future and an emergency cushion, breaking any cycle of money chaos, and modelling steadier habits for their children.

### Persona 3: Late-diagnosed adults (24s to 45s)

- **Motivation:** They finally understand why money has always been hard (brain wiring, not laziness), and they want a system that works with their brain instead of one more thing they fail at. The real driver is relief from years of shame and a desire to stop feeling "bad with money" and rebuild self-trust.
- **Situational goal:** Get a grip on income that keeps vanishing, shut down impulse buys, forgotten subscriptions, and late fees, and finally build the emergency cushion they never managed before, using a system that does not run on willpower or memory.
- **Life goal:** Long-term stability and freedom on their own terms (a home, travel, security), and a durable sense of being in control of their money and their life.

## 4. Known Limitation and Proposed Approach

### The limitation

The product cannot reliably detect a purchase before it happens. If it only reads bank data through open banking, it sees a transaction after the card is authorized, not before. It has no hook into third-party shopping apps or their checkouts, and it cannot see an in-person impulse buy, such as walking past a shop and buying on the spot. So any feature described as prompting the user "before they buy" has to be understood within that limit.

### Proposed approach

Rather than trying to intercept every purchase, the realistic plan is to reduce how much damage an impulse buy can do, and to prompt at the moments that matter. Listed in order of how realistic they are:

1. **Bucket money ahead of time, and only leave what is safe to spend.** The moment income lands, ring-fence the essentials (food, rent, bills) into separate buckets, so the only money available to impulse-spend is already the safe-to-spend amount. This caps the damage structurally instead of relying on catching the user in the act. This is the strongest option and it is already proven in practice from lived experience.
2. **Analyse spending patterns to find high-risk days, then prompt automatically.** Track when impulse buys usually happen (for example, payday or certain days of the week) and send an automatic notification ahead of those moments, rather than trying to detect a single purchase. This is a heads-up based on the user's own patterns, not on reading the shop.

The other options are still worth keeping on the table, but are less central:

- **Near-instant alerts the moment a purchase posts,** fast enough to interrupt a spending streak even if the first buy cannot be stopped.
- **Issuing the app's own card or account,** the only way to truly pause a purchase in real time, but a large increase in scope (becoming or partnering with a regulated payment provider).
- **Optional location-based (geofencing) nudges** near known trigger stores, kept off by default given the privacy and tone trade-offs.
